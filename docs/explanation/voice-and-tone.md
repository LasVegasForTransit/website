# Voice and tone

Read this before drafting any LVBT copy.

LVBT's voice is **principled but sharp**:

- **Direct.** Short sentences. Active voice. Don't bury the point.
- **Confident.** State the case; don't hedge into uselessness.
- **Pointed when warranted.** When the casino lobby blocked airport rail, say so. When the Vegas Loop is a bad solution, say why. Don't be edgy for its own sake — be specific and accurate.
- **Coalition-ready.** Avoid language that makes future partners (labor, faith communities, sympathetic businesses, RTC staff, legislators) impossible to work with.

What this is **not**:

- Not "fellow kids" cringe.
- Not the academic-policy-paper voice. ("Stakeholders" is rarely the right word.)
- Not the corporate-nonprofit voice. ("Empowering" and "centering" are usually doing less work than people think.)
- Not feed-style doom-posting. We are organizing for outcomes, not vibes.

When in doubt, ask: _"Would this read well at a Senate Revenue Committee hearing AND at a coalition meeting in a union hall?"_ If yes, ship it.

## Founder voice patterns

Distilled from Willie's April 2026 founder letter — the canonical reference for LVBT's first-person voice. The unifying move is **concrete-then-frame**: lead with a specific lived moment; let the thesis arrive as its inevitable conclusion. Almost never start with the thesis.

### Personal grounding

- **First-person anecdotes anchored to specific time, place, and self-deprecating physical detail.** Not _"we believe transit matters"_ — _"In summer 2019 I flew to DFW … trying to haul a backpack, a carry-on, and a checked luggage collectively weighing more than myself through two trains and a bus transfer over two hours was kind of insane, even for a regular transit user."_ Specific moments do the persuasive work that abstract argument usually does.
- **Place-loyalty as the personal stake.** _"I felt a little embarrassed that my city,"_ _"the children who will grow up here."_ The advocacy is rooted in being a Vegan, not in abstract civic duty.

### Frank framing

- **Candor about the odds, used as a credibility marker.** _"borderline impossible,"_ _"Frankly, I'm unsure whether … a lot of our vision is doable within a generational timespan."_ Undersell while still committing — the mismatch is the voice. Most founder copy oversells; ours doesn't.
- **"Frankly" as a recurring sincerity tic.** Reads as "dropping the polish for a second."
- **Vegas-scoped honesty about what's possible.** _"We live in the Mojave Desert. Pushing for New York City-style subway and density here is likely not practical."_ Refuses to pretend Vegas is somewhere else.
- **Acknowledge-then-reframe.** _"Many urbanists … are right. However …"_ Engage the critique before moving past it; never dismiss a counterargument unaddressed.

### Rhetorical engines

- **Anaphoric stacks of concrete wants.** _"I want tree-lined bike paths. I want bus stops with shade. I want buildings on streets that invite me and my friends to stay in … I want to see hubs instead of strip malls."_ And _"It's time we X. It's time we Y. It's time we fight."_ Lists of concrete wants in repeated grammar.
- **Stacked semicolon lists with concrete examples.** _"neighborhoods where it's often 5–10 times quicker to drive than to walk to the grocery store; where seniors who can't drive are effectively trapped in their homes; and where children regularly get injured or killed trying to cross the street."_ Three escalating specifics, semicolon-separated.
- **External authority as moral anchor — same form, used twice.** Gustavo Petro: _"A developed country is not a place where the poor have cars. It's where the rich use public transportation."_ Once as a teaching moment, again at the close.
- **Long compound sentences punctuated by short declaratives.** A packed sentence with semicolons and qualifications, then _"But it was possible."_ Short ones land like punctuation marks.

### Register

- **Startup-meets-civic vocabulary.** _"moonshot thinking,"_ _"more of a mission-driven startup that's willing to think creatively."_ Tech/founder vocabulary imported into nonprofit advocacy without irony.
- **Class lens on transit.** _"Transit isn't just 'something poor people have to use.'"_ Reframes political valence: transit as freedom-for-everyone.
- **Generational time-scale.** _"truly generational, transformational change,"_ _"the children who will grow up here,"_ _"generational timespan."_ The argument is sized in lifetimes.
- **"Oasis in the desert"** as the destination image — the antonym of _"a city that caters to tourists."_ Recurring metaphor.
- **Pun-aware playfulness, used sparingly.** _"The road ahead (pun intended) is anything but simple."_ _"All aboard for Las Vegans for Better Transit."_ Light, not twee.
- **Self-deprecating asides paired with anecdotes.** _"kind of insane, even for a regular transit user,"_ _"I confused myself into taking a train headed to Brooklyn."_ The founder voice is never above the reader.

## Overselling vs. anchored opinion

Distinct things — easy to confuse, but only one of them violates the voice.

- **Overselling is vague hyperbole with no anchor.** _"the moment,"_ _"the time is now,"_ _"closes the window,"_ _"transformational change"_ used as filler, _"we're rewriting the rules,"_ _"the most ambitious effort ever undertaken."_ Drop these.
- **Positional opinion grounded in measurable standards is on-voice.** _"world-class public transportation,"_ _"the safest place to bike in the Southwest,"_ _"a system Phoenix or Salt Lake City would recognize as functional."_ These name a target measurable against external benchmarks (peer cities, frequency, mode share, fatality rates). They state a normative position the org will defend — not a vague feel-good claim.

**Test:** can the claim be checked against an external standard? If yes, it's anchored opinion — keep it. If no, it's overselling — replace with something specific.

## Specific patterns to avoid

- **Technocratic urbanism vocab as the lead.** Mixed-use, mode share, TOD, VMT, frequency hours — these belong in body text or captions, never in headlines or ledes.
- **We-believe / we-are-committed boilerplate.** State what we'll do, not what we feel about doing it.
- **Abstractions without specific examples following.** Every general claim should be followed by a named instance within two sentences.
- **Generic CTAs as button labels.** _Get involved_, _Take action_, _Learn more_ are lazy. Use specific verbs: Subscribe, RSVP, Donate, Read the brief.
- **Slogans on utility pages.** _"Show up. Things change."_ on /events is the wrong job for a calendar listing. Plain page-job nouns belong on utility pages; slogans, when used at all, belong on persuasion pages.
- **Templated card grammar.** Three identical eyebrow → headline → body cards in a row reads AI-generated. Vary composition deliberately.
